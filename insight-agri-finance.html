Financing a season: cash-flow planning for smallholder agribusiness

Inputs in November, revenue in June. A practical framework for surviving the gap without expensive emergency credit.

JB By Jay C. O. Banda, Founder & Lead Financial Planner 9 August 2026

A farming season is not a monthly business. Inputs are bought in one lump, labour is paid in bursts, and revenue arrives months later, often all at once. A budget that treats every month the same will run out of money in February — precisely when the crop needs one more pass.

Start with the cash calendar, not the crop budget

List every month of the season down the left of a page. Across the top, put four columns: opening cash, money in, money out, closing cash. Then fill it honestly, using last season's records and this season's prices. Most farmers discover two things immediately: the lowest cash month (usually January to March), and the size of the gap they need to bridge.

That gap has a number. If it is MWK 400,000, you can plan for MWK 400,000. If it is vague, you will borrow at the worst possible moment at the worst possible terms.

Separate the three costs

  • Direct inputs — seed, fertiliser, chemicals, hired labour for land prep and harvest.
  • Fixed overheads — land rent, equipment maintenance, permits, storage, family drawings.
  • Financing costs — interest, fees, and the cost of any delayed payment.

Most smallholder budgets track only the first. The second and third are what turn a profitable season into a break-even one. Include them before you decide a crop is worth planting.

Price the season at three yields

Build the plan at your expected yield, then at 70% of it, then at 50%. If the business only works at the optimistic number, the structure is too fragile. Look at what changes: can you reduce hired labour? Plant a shorter-cycle crop on part of the land? Contract storage in advance at a fixed rate rather than paying spot prices in a shortage?

The goal is not to predict the season. It is to build something that survives a bad one.

Structure credit to the harvest, not to the calendar

Short-term facilities priced monthly can be brutal when they are taken for a six-month production cycle. Ask three questions before signing:

  1. What is the all-in cost? Interest plus fees plus any compulsory insurance or savings requirement, expressed as a single figure for the whole period.
  2. When is repayment due? If the first instalment lands before your harvest, the facility is mis-structured for your cycle.
  3. What happens if the harvest is late? A rollover or grace period is worth more than a marginally lower rate.

Where possible, match repayment to the buyer's payment terms. A contract with an off-taker who pays in July should not be financed by a facility that demands money in April.

Buy inputs early; buy them in writing

Input prices tend to move against the buyer as the season approaches. Pre-season purchase, group buying through a cooperative, or an agreed forward price with a supplier all reduce the cash spike. Get the agreement in writing, including delivery dates and replacement terms for defective stock. Verbal promises are not a supply chain.

Keep a reserve — even a small one

Target 10% of the season's total cost held in reserve. It sounds impossible in a tight year. Start at 3% and build. The reserve is not idle money; it is what stops a small problem — a pump failure, a delayed delivery, a funeral — from becoming an emergency loan at 30% a month.

Record the season while it is happening Bags harvested, bags sold, price per bag, labour paid, transport paid. Ten minutes a week. Next season's plan is only as good as this season's records, and lenders who see two years of records treat you as a different class of borrower.

When to seek advice

If the cash calendar shows a gap you cannot cover, or if a lender's repayment schedule does not line up with your harvest, get a second opinion before you sign. Restructuring a facility is far easier before it goes into arrears than after.
