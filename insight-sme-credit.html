Seven documents that decide your SME loan application

Lenders rarely reject a good business. They reject an incomplete file. Here is the paperwork that moves you to the front of the queue.

CP By Chikondi Phiri, SME Finance Specialist 28 August 2026

In most rejections we review, the business is viable. The file is not. A credit officer has roughly twenty minutes to understand your business, assess risk, and justify a decision to a committee. Your documents are doing the arguing for you — or failing to.

1. Twelve months of bank statements, in the business name

This is the most common avoidable failure. If business takings run through a personal wallet or a joint account with school fees and rent, the lender cannot separate trading performance from household spending. Open a dedicated business account, route all sales through it, and keep personal transfers clearly labelled. Do that for six months before applying and your assessed turnover changes materially.

Print the statements. Highlight the salary you pay yourself, if any. Lenders are not suspicious of an owner being paid — they are suspicious of an owner who cannot show how much.

2. Management accounts, not just year-end financials

Year-end accounts tell a lender what happened up to eighteen months ago. Management accounts — a simple profit and loss plus a balance sheet, monthly, reconciled — tell them what is happening now. You do not need software. A spreadsheet with consistent categories, updated monthly, is enough. Inconsistency is the killer: if "transport" means fuel in one month and a vehicle purchase in another, the numbers are not comparable.

3. A 12-month cash-flow forecast you can defend

Most rejected forecasts are too optimistic and too round. A credible forecast states its assumptions: expected volume growth, price changes, debtor days, stock turn, and the timing of any seasonal dip. If your business is seasonal, show the trough and show how the facility covers it. Lenders are far more comfortable with a forecast that anticipates a hard month than with one that assumes smooth growth.

A lender is not buying your optimism. They are buying evidence that you understand your own cash cycle.

4. Six months of supplier and customer evidence

Invoices, purchase orders, contracts, delivery notes, or a mobile money merchant statement. The purpose is proof of a trading relationship that existed before the loan. Three repeat customers with documented monthly orders are more persuasive than a list of forty prospects.

5. A debt schedule — including informal debt

List every facility: bank loans, mobile loans, supplier credit, family loans, hire purchase. Include the monthly instalment and the outstanding balance. Undisclosed obligations discovered later are treated as a misrepresentation, even when the omission was casual. If you are heavily leveraged, say so and bring the plan to reduce it; hiding it ends the conversation permanently.

6. Identification, registration and tax compliance

Certificate of incorporation or business registration, TIN, recent tax returns or a compliance letter, directors' IDs and proof of address. These are unglamorous and they stop a file dead. Check the expiry dates. A file that is complete but for a lapsed document sits in a queue until somebody notices, and by then your season has moved on.

7. A one-page summary that a committee can read

Attach a short cover sheet: what the business does, what the money is for, how much is needed, the repayment source, and the primary risk with your mitigation. Keep it to one page. The credit officer will use it to brief the committee. Give them a clean sentence and you have influenced the meeting you were not invited to.

What "loan-ready" actually looks like

Our rule of thumb: a lender should be able to approve you without asking a single clarifying question. If they have to call you for statements, the application loses a week; if they have to call twice, it often loses momentum altogether.

Before you submit Read your own file as if you were the credit officer, and ask one question: "Would I lend to this business on this evidence?" If the honest answer is "not yet", you already know what to fix.
